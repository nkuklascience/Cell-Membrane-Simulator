Background

How do cells control what gets in and out?

Every second, trillions of microscopic cells in your body are constantly working to keep you alive and healthy. To survive, your cells must carefully control what enters and exits across their cell membrane, maintaining a balanced internal environment known as dynamic equilibrium.

Small molecules like oxygen and carbon dioxide move freely into and out of cells from areas of high concentration to low concentration through diffusion. Water also moves across membranes to balance solute concentrations through a specialized form of passive water movement called osmosis. Because diffusion and osmosis require no energy from the cell, both are forms of passive transport.

However, larger or charged particles—such as glucose—cannot pass directly through the fatty membrane on their own. Instead, they rely on facilitated diffusion, using specialized protein channels to enter the cell from high to low concentration without consuming cellular energy. When cells need to push materials against their concentration gradient (from low to high concentration), they must use active transport, which requires the cell to spend energy (ATP).

Real-world connection

Cystic Fibrosis

The cells lining your lungs have a protein channel called CFTR that lets chloride ions (Cl⁻) leave the cell. Water follows the chloride out by osmosis, which keeps the mucus in your airways thin and watery. In people with cystic fibrosis, a gene mutation makes the CFTR protein the wrong shape.

Try moving chloride ions out of the cell with a healthy channel, then switch to a misfolded channel. Watch the mucus layer.
